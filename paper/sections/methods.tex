This section briefly summarizes the clustering algorithms evaluated in our experimental survey.
We organize methods primarily by \emph{optimization paradigm}, distinguishing between \emph{classical} combinatorial optimization methods and \emph{learning-based} approaches with trainable parameters.
Classical methods are further grouped according to their dominant optimization mechanism (e.g., greedy agglomeration, multilevel local optimization, and metaheuristics/ensembles), as well as by computational setting or guarantees (streaming algorithms and exact solvers).
Learning-based methods are grouped by the role modularity plays in training, distinguishing between methods that directly optimize continuous or differentiable relaxations of modularity and those that use modularity as a guiding signal within contrastive or infomax objectives.
Within each subgroup, algorithms are listed chronologically.
Unless stated otherwise, methods aim to maximize modularity; we additionally include a small number of widely used non-modularity baselines (e.g., random-walk- and flow-based methods) for contextual comparison.

\subsection{Classical Modularity Optimization Methods}

\subsubsection{Greedy agglomeration and multilevel local search}

\paragraph{\textsc{CNM} (Clauset--Newman--Moore)}
\textsc{CNM} is a hierarchical agglomerative algorithm that greedily maximizes modularity, starting from singleton communities (each vertex assigned to its own cluster) and repeatedly merging pairs of communities with the largest modularity gain $\Delta Q$. 
%It maintains the quantities $e_{ij}$ (the fraction of edges between communities $i$ and $j$) and $a_i$ (the fraction of edge ends incident to community $i$), yielding the modularity expression $Q = \sum_i (e_{ii} - a_i^2)$, and computes $\Delta Q_{ij}$ only for community pairs connected by at least one edge. 
% Efficient bookkeeping yields near-linear behavior on large sparse graphs, making \textsc{CNM} a standard scalable baseline for greedy modularity maximization.
To achieve scalability, \textsc{CNM} computes $\Delta Q_{c_i,c_j}$ only for community pairs $(c_i,c_j)$ connected by at least one edge, and stores these values in balanced binary trees and max-heaps, allowing efficient updates after each merge. 
The algorithm produces a dendrogram that represents a hierarchical decomposition of the network into communities at all levels, and returns the clustering attaining maximum modularity. 
Its running time is $O(md \log n)$, where $d$ is the dendrogram depth.

\paragraph{\textsc{WT} (Wakita--Tsurumi)}
\textsc{WT} is a refinement of \textsc{CNM}, motivated by the observation that \textsc{CNM} can produce highly unbalanced merges that degrade scalability on large-scale graphs.
The algorithm preserves the hierarchical agglomerative structure of \textsc{CNM}, but modifies the merge-selection rule to consider both the modularity gain $\Delta Q_{c_i,c_j}$ and a community-size ratio $\mathrm{ratio}(c_i,c_j)$.
This additional size-aware criterion biases the algorithm toward more balanced agglomerations.

\paragraph{\textsc{Louvain}}
\textsc{Louvain} is a multilevel greedy algorithm that replaces \textsc{CNM}'s community-pair merging with local vertex moves.
Instead of selecting pairs of communities $(c_i,c_j)$ to merge based on $\Delta Q_{c_i,c_j}$, Louvain iteratively moves individual vertices $u$ to neighboring communities $c$ whenever the induced modularity gain $\Delta Q_{u \rightarrow c}$ is positive.
Once no improving vertex move exists, each community is contracted into a supernode and the process is repeated on the induced graph.
Each local-moving phase runs in linear time in the number of edges, and the number of multilevel passes is typically small, yielding linear overall runtime in practice.

\paragraph{\textsc{Leiden}}
\textsc{Leiden} extends \textsc{Louvain} by adding a refinement phase that splits and restructures communities before contraction to address the possibility of disconnected or weakly connected communities produced by \textsc{Louvain}.
After the local-moving phase, each community is refined independently by starting from singleton nodes and merging vertices only with subcommunities inside the original community, provided this yields a positive modularity gain and sufficient internal connectivity.
Refinement merges are non-greedy and selected probabilistically among improving options, enabling broader exploration of the search space while never decreasing modularity.
As a result, Leiden guarantees that all output communities are connected and locally optimal with respect to vertex moves, while preserving the multilevel modularity-maximization framework.

\subsubsection{Non-modularity baselines (random-walk / flow objectives)}

\paragraph{\textsc{Walktrap}}
\textsc{Walktrap} is a hierarchical clustering algorithm that departs from modularity optimization and instead operates on the intuition that random walks on a graph tend to get "trapped" into densely connected regions corresponding to communities.
Each vertex is characterized by the probability distribution over vertices reached by a $t$-step random walk starting from it, and structural similarity between vertices (and communities) is defined by comparing these distributions. 
A distance defined on these distributions yields a computable similarity measure between vertices and communities.
Communities are then obtained by agglomerative merging of the closest pairs under this distance, producing a dendrogram similar to \textsc{CNM}.
The algorithm runs in $O(m n d)$ time, where $d$ is the height of the resulting dendrogram.
% Walktrap defines a random-walk-based similarity between vertices: short walks tend to remain inside dense regions, so vertices with similar walk distributions are likely in the same community. It then performs agglomerative clustering using this distance, producing a dendrogram from which a partition can be selected.

\paragraph{\textsc{Infomap}}
Instead of comparing random-walk distributions as in \textsc{Walktrap}, \textsc{Infomap} seeks a clustering that minimizes the expected description length of a random walk, as formalized by the map equation. This objective is rooted in the minimum description length principle, treating community detection as a data compression problem: the optimal partition is the one that allows for the shortest possible "code" to describe the walker's path. \textsc{Infomap}'s search mechanism is a greedy stochastic multilevel heuristic inspired by the \textsc{Louvain} algorithm, but it incorporates additional fine-tuning and coarse-tuning steps to avoid local minima, similar to the refinements later introduced by \textsc{Leiden}. The algorithm operates in two primary phases: a two-level phase that starts with singleton nodes and iteratively moves them to neighboring modules to reduce codelength, followed by a multilevel phase that recursively groups these modules to identify hierarchical structures.

\subsubsection{Metaheuristics and ensemble/hybrid search}

\paragraph{\textsc{CGGCi-RG}}
\textsc{CGGCi-RG} combines a randomized greedy modularity optimizer with an ensemble-based consolidation scheme known as Core Groups Graph Clustering (\textsc{CGGC}).
\textsc{CGGC} extracts \emph{core groups} of vertices that are repeatedly assigned to the same community across multiple high-quality clusterings and contracts them into supernodes to stabilize and refine the solution.
The underlying Randomized Greedy (RG) algorithm follows a \textsc{CNM}-style hierarchical merging process, but restricts merge selection by sampling a fixed number of candidate communities and choosing the best merge among them.
Multiple independent RG runs are executed to generate a diverse set of high-quality dendrograms.
From these runs, core groups are identified and contracted, and modularity optimization is then repeated on the reduced graph.

\paragraph{\textsc{VNS}}
\textsc{VNS} applies a \emph{Variable Neighborhood Search} metaheuristic to modularity maximization.
Starting from an initial clustering, the algorithm repeatedly selects a subproblem induced by a randomly chosen community and a growing set of neighboring communities, and perturbs the solution using one of several predefined cluster-level shaking operators (e.g., division, fusion, redistribution).
Each perturbed solution is refined using a modularity-based local search (LPAm+), first within the subproblem and, upon improvement, on the full graph.
If no improvement is found, the neighborhood size is increased; otherwise it is reset, allowing systematic escape from local optima while maintaining focus on modularity optimization.
%VNS applies variable-neighborhood search to modularity maximization: it alternates between diversification (``shaking'' moves that perturb the current partition) and intensification via local improvement. By systematically changing the neighborhood structure, VNS escapes local optima more effectively than purely greedy local-move methods.

\paragraph{\textsc{COMBO}}
\textsc{COMBO} is a general-purpose community-detection optimizer that performs a broader search over partitions than \textsc{Louvain} style single-vertex moves. 
Starting from an initial clustering, the algorithm iteratively considers pairs of source and destination communities and computes the best redistribution of vertices between them.
For each such pair, \textsc{COMBO} performs a Kernighan--Lin style sequence of vertex moves, starting from multiple initial configurations (including full merges and partial random transfers), and selects the configuration yielding the largest modularity gain.
The best improving recombination is applied at each iteration, and the process repeats until no further improvement in modularity is possible. The algorithm runs in $O(n^2 C)$ time, where $C$ is the number of clusters. 

\paragraph{\textsc{VieClus}}
\textsc{VieClus} is a memetic (evolutionary) algorithm for modularity maximization that optimizes a population of candidate clusterings rather than a single solution.
In a memetic framework, each clustering is treated as an individual, and new solutions are generated by recombination and mutation: recombination exploits common structure in high-quality solutions, while mutation introduces controlled randomness to maintain diversity and escape local optima.
VieClus initializes a diverse population using an adapted \textsc{Louvain} method, and generates offspring via \emph{overlay clustering}, where two vertices are grouped together only if they co-occur in both parent solutions.
The resulting solution is refined using \textsc{Louvain} or multilevel local search, while mutation is realized by splitting clusters via graph partitioning before recombination.
Offspring solutions replace similar population members only if they achieve at least equal modularity, ensuring both solution quality and population diversity. After a set time limit, \textsc{VieClus} returns the best clustering found thus far. 

\subsubsection{Streaming}

\paragraph{\textsc{Hollocou}}
\textsc{Hollocou} is a one-pass streaming algorithm for community detection based on the observation that, under random edge orderings, intra-community edges tend to arrive earlier than inter-community edges.
The algorithm starts from the singleton clustering and processes edges sequentially.
For an arriving edge $(u,v)$, \textsc{Hollocou} decides whether to merge the communities of $u$ and $v$ based on their current community volumes.
If both volumes are below a fixed threshold, the node belonging to the smaller-volume community joins the other; otherwise, no merge is performed.
The authors show that this local, volume-based merging rule corresponds to a greedy optimization of modularity in the streaming setting.

\paragraph{\textsc{CluStRE}}
\textsc{CluStRE} follows the node-streaming model unlike the edge-streaming model of \textsc{Hollocou}. Here, nodes arrive sequentially together with their neighborhood lists.
Starting from singleton clustering, each node $v$ is assigned to the cluster among its neighbors that maximizes the modularity gain $\Delta Q$, similar to a \textsc{Louvain} local move.
During streaming, a quotient graph is constructed whose nodes represent clusters and whose weighted edges summarize inter- and intra-cluster connectivity, preserving modularity exactly.
This quotient graph is optionally refined using \textsc{VieClus}.
The graph may be re-streamed to perform further local improvements, iterating until modularity converges.

\subsubsection{Exact Solvers}

\paragraph{\textsc{Bayan}}
\textsc{Bayan} is an exact algorithm for modularity maximization based on a branch-and-cut formulation of an integer program with binary decision variables encoding community co-membership.
It enforces consistent cluster assignments using triangular constraints on node triples, which branch on whether three nodes must belong to the same community or cannot all be clustered together.
Upper bounds are obtained by solving linear programming relaxations of the formulation, while lower bounds are computed from feasible clusterings produced by \textsc{COMBO}.
Through branching on violated triangular constraints, the algorithm modifies the problem by either contracting node triples into a supernode or discouraging their co-assignment until optimality is certified or a prescribed gap is reached.

\paragraph{\textsc{MaxSAT}}
The \textsc{MaxSAT} approach formulates modularity maximization as a weighted partial MaxSAT problem.
Binary decision variables indicate whether pairs of vertices belong to the same community, while hard clauses enforce transitivity constraints to ensure a valid clustering.
Weighted soft clauses encode pairwise contributions to modularity, such that maximizing the total satisfied weight is equivalent to maximizing modularity.
To improve scalability, the formulation uses a sparse encoding that omits redundant transitivity constraints without affecting optimality.
Solving the resulting MaxSAT instance yields a clustering with provably optimal modularity.

\subsection{Learning-Based Methods}

\subsubsection{Continuous/Differentiable modularity maximization}

\paragraph{\textsc{DMoN} (Deep Modularity Networks)} \textsc{DMoN} introduces an unsupervised modularity-based objective and realizes it using a GNN for graph clustering. 
A GNN followed by a softmax function produces soft cluster assignments, which are optimized using a fully differentiable objective whose main term corresponds to modularity in spectral form. 
The objective further includes a \emph{collapse regularization} term that prevents trivial solutions in which all nodes are assigned to the same cluster.

\paragraph{\textsc{GNNS}.}
\textsc{GNNS} is a GNN-style method for unsupervised network clustering via modularity optimization, inspired by recurrent GNN models.
Unlike discrete modularity heuristics, \textsc{GNNS} formulates community detection as a continuous optimization process over soft cluster assignments.
Starting from random initial assignments, the algorithm iteratively updates each node’s soft cluster assignment by combining its previous assignment with aggregated assignment information from neighboring nodes, weighted by the modularity matrix.
Each update is followed by a normalization step to maintain valid assignment distributions, and simple activation functions are used to amplify assignments supported by positive modularity contributions while suppressing weaker ones.
The procedure is repeated for multiple random initializations and parameter choices; intermediate solutions are filtered based on achieved modularity, and the final clustering is obtained by discretizing the converged soft cluster assignments.

\paragraph{\textsc{DGCluster}}
\textsc{DGCluster} is a fully differentiable method for deep graph clustering based on graph structure and node attributes. Unlike most learning-based approaches including \textsc{DMoN}, \textsc{DGCluster} does not require the number of communities to be predefined. 
It first computes node embeddings using a GNN that incorporates graph structure and node attributes.
Similarities between node embeddings are then interpreted as soft pairwise community assignments and used to parameterize the modularity objective.
This objective is optimized in a differentiable manner to train the GNN parameters, after which final community assignments are obtained by clustering the learned node embeddings using another algorithm called \textsc{BIRCH}. The overall complexity scales linearly with the graph size. 

\paragraph{\textsc{LIM}}
\textsc{LIM} (Less Is More) is a learning-based community detection method that integrates global structural information, local graph structure, and node attributes within a modularity maximization framework.
It first performs community pre-detection using a \textsc{Louvain} to extract global structural communities, which are summarized as structural community centers in a feature space.
Additionally, a graph convolutional network is used to learn node representations that capture local structural information and node attributes.
Soft cluster assignments are then computed by measuring similarity between node representations and the structural community centers, integrating global structure with local structural and attribute information.
Finally, a modularity maximization objective defined over these soft cluster memberships is optimized to obtain the final community assignment.

\subsubsection{Contrastive / infomax objectives aligned with modularity}

\paragraph{\textsc{CommDGI}}
\textsc{CommDGI} (Community Deep Graph Infomax) is a representation-learning approach to community detection that learns soft community assignments by maximizing mutual information.
Building on Deep Graph Infomax, it jointly maximizes \emph{graph mutual information} between node and graph representations and \emph{community mutual information} between node and community (subgraph) representations to encode the graph and clustering feature of each node.
A trainable clustering layer is integrated into the GNN, enabling end-to-end training for community detection.
Unlike learning-based methods that directly optimize modularity (e.g., \textsc{DMoN}, \textsc{DGCluster}, \textsc{LIM}), modularity is used here as an auxiliary objective together with mutual information, via a soft $k$-means–style clustering layer, to further encourage structurally coherent communities.

\paragraph{\textsc{UCoDe}}
\textsc{UCoDe} (Unified Community Detection) is a GNN-based community detection method that jointly supports non-overlapping and overlapping communities within a single unsupervised framework.
Like \textsc{DMoN} and \textsc{DGCluster}, it represents communities through soft cluster assignments produced by a GNN, but unlike these methods it does not rely on direct modularity maximization alone.
Instead, \textsc{UCoDe} optimizes a single contrastive objective derived from the modularity matrix, which simultaneously encourages high intra-community similarity and low inter-community similarity at the community level.
This contrastive formulation allows \textsc{UCoDe} to recover non-overlapping communities while naturally supporting overlap, without requiring separate objectives or post-processing.
Compared to combined-objective methods such as \textsc{CommDGI}, \textsc{UCoDe} uses a single, interpretable loss and requires minimal hyperparameter tuning.

\paragraph{\textsc{MAGI}}
\textsc{MAGI} establishes a formal connection between modularity maximization and graph contrastive learning by showing that modularity naturally defines positive and negative node pairs via the sign of the modularity matrix.
Specifically, node pairs with positive modularity coefficients are treated as positive samples, while pairs with non-positive coefficients are treated as negatives, yielding a contrastive objective equivalent to relaxed modularity maximization.
Based on this insight, \textsc{MAGI} uses modularity maximization as a community-aware pretext task to train a GNN encoder with a softmax-based SimCLR contrastive loss, without relying on graph augmentations.
To achieve scalability and capture high-order proximity, \textsc{MAGI} constructs mini-batch modularity matrices using a two-stage random-walk sampling procedure.
