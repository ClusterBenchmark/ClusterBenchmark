We surveyed over twenty modularity-based graph clustering algorithms, spanning classical heuristic methods, parallel implementations, and recent learning-based approaches. When modularity maximization is the sole objective, the results are clear: \textsc{VieClus} consistently achieves the highest modularity scores, provided sufficient memory is available. \textsc{Leiden}, an improved variant of \textsc{Louvain}, follows closely in terms of solution quality while offering substantially faster runtimes. This difference can be attributed in part to the search-based nature of \textsc{VieClus}, which requires users to explicitly specify a time budget, whereas \textsc{Leiden} employs a greedy optimization strategy.

Beyond modularity alone, different algorithms exhibit distinct strengths. For example, the streaming method \textsc{CluStRE} achieves extremely low runtimes and memory consumption, making it attractive for large-scale or resource-constrained settings. Learning-based methods, such as \textsc{GNNS}, demonstrate that neural approaches are approaching the performance of manually designed heuristics. However, they generally require significantly more memory and do not yet achieve competitive modularity values. At the same time, there is growing interest in combining modularity optimization with auxiliary objectives to recover ground-truth communities better. In this setting, methods that exploit node features can be advantageous, such as \textsc{MAGI} that consistently achieves the best recovery of ground-truth clusters in our experiments. In contrast, approaches such as \textsc{DMoN} remain effective when it is unclear whether community structure is primarily encoded in the graph topology or in node attributes.

Finally, to facilitate future experimental research in this area, we provide a unified interface that enables direct, reproducible comparisons across a wide range of modularity-based clustering algorithms. We hope that this framework lowers the barrier for evaluating new methods against established baselines and encourages more systematic experimental studies of graph clustering algorithms.
