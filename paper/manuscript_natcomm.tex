\begin{abstract}
  \noindent
  Modularity maximization is one of the most widely used objective functions for community detection in networks, balancing intra-community connectivity against a random null model. Over two decades, algorithmic development has split into two largely isolated research communities: classical discrete optimization heuristics operating on topology alone, and deep learning architectures, primarily graph neural networks (GNNs), that integrate node attributes into continuous modularity relaxations. Because each line of work evaluates almost exclusively against baselines within its own paradigm, practical trade-offs among optimization quality, scalability, and hyperparameter sensitivity remain unexplored. Here, we present a systematic experimental benchmark evaluating 17 representative graph clustering algorithms, centered on modularity maximization alongside key non-modularity reference baselines across seven algorithmic paradigms (greedy agglomerative, multilevel, memetic, streaming, flow/spectral, exact integer linear programming, and neural/GNNs). We evaluate methods across real-world attributed networks, large-scale pure topology benchmarks up to 3.3 billion edges, and synthetic graphs under controlled topological and attribute signal degradation. Across three standardized evaluation protocols isolating out-of-the-box defaults, hardware acceleration, and hyperparameter tuning, our findings reveal clear trade-offs: classical heuristics (notably \textsc{VieClus} and \textsc{Leiden}) dominate pure modularity and scalability without parameter tuning, while GNN-based methods excel in ground-truth community recovery when informative node attributes exist. However, GNNs require heavy hyperparameter tuning, have lower memory ceilings due to accelerator VRAM constraints, and suffer substantial performance drops as attributes degrade. We additionally contribute a unified, open-source framework for interfacing all the evaluated clustering algorithms, supporting reproducibility and future benchmarking.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Networks are useful in modeling complex systems across the natural sciences, social sciences, and engineering. Such systems are represented as graphs, where vertices denote entities and edges encode interactions between them, as in social networks, communication infrastructures, biological and financial systems, and information networks. A common feature of real-world graphs is \emph{community structure}, where vertices form groups with dense internal connectivity and sparser external connections.

Identifying these groups, commonly termed \emph{graph clustering} or \emph{community detection}, is a central task in network analysis, with applications ranging from social and political analysis and fraud detection~\cite{Akoglu2015} to bioinformatics~\cite{Cai2011,Xu2002}, program optimization~\cite{Demme2012,McFarling89}, and epidemic modeling~\cite{Newman2003}. Beyond application-specific insights, community structure reveals fundamental organizational patterns, such as cohesive subgraphs, boundary vertices, and inter-community interactions. Despite extensive study, no single clustering method performs best across all network types~\cite{Peel2016}, motivating continued algorithmic development and comparative evaluation.

Most community-detection methods formulate clustering as an optimization problem over vertex partitions, assigning a quality score to each candidate solution. 
Among clustering objectives, \emph{modularity}~\cite{girvan2002,Newman2006}, which measures the density of edges inside communities as compared to edges between communities while accounting for random chance, is widely adopted and shown to be effective~\cite{dimacschallengegraphpartandcluster,licommunitydetectionsurvey24}. Thus, this benchmark focuses on modularity-based methods, while including widely used non-modularity baselines. 

We distinguish two broad classes of modularity-based algorithms. \emph{Classical} methods rely on discrete optimization heuristics driven solely by graph topology and a well-defined objective function, yielding interpretable optimization behavior. In contrast, \emph{learning-based} approaches integrate modularity into deep learning pipelines, most commonly via graph neural networks, incorporating node attributes alongside graph topology through feature representations. This added expressiveness introduces trade-offs in interpretability, training complexity, and computational cost~\cite{neuromap2024}.

Community detection faces challenges arising from the scale, complexity and frequently-updating content of real-world graphs. Effective methods must balance solution quality, often with noisy data lacking ground truth, with runtime and memory scalability. Sensitivity to parameters, such as assumed cluster counts, further complicates practical deployment~\cite{Wani2024}. Understanding how different algorithms navigate these trade-offs remains an open challenge.

Despite existing surveys~\cite{Liu2020, Jin2023, survey2023, Li2024, liang2024, Wani2024, Messias2025}, there is no comprehensive experimental study that jointly evaluates classical and learning-based modularity optimization methods under a unified framework. This paper addresses this gap with the following:

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
	\item We present the first large-scale experimental benchmark that jointly evaluates classical heuristics and modern learning-based algorithms across 17 representative modularity-based clustering methods.
	\item We release a unified open-source experimental framework that adapts both classical and learning-based methods behind a common interface, enabling reproducible benchmarking and future research.
	\item We establish a conceptual taxonomy and comparative survey organizing modularity-based algorithms across seven optimization paradigms, detailing how classical discrete heuristics and deep GNN architectures formulate and optimize modularity objectives.
	\item We provide comprehensive empirical insights across three standardized evaluation protocols (default settings, hardware acceleration, and per-instance hyperparameter tuning) evaluated on real-world attributed graphs, large-scale benchmarks up to 3.3 billion edges, and synthetic graphs under controlled signal degradation. Our findings uncover significant trade-offs: classical heuristics (\textsc{VieClus} and \textsc{Leiden}) dominate pure modularity and scalability without tuning, whereas GNNs excel when attributes align with ground truth but incur a steep hyperparameter tuning tax and strict accelerator VRAM capacity limits.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preliminary}\label{sec:preliminary}

\paragraph*{\textbf{Graphs and clusterings}.}
An \emph{(attributed) graph} is defined as $G = (V, E, \mathcal{F})$, where $V = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ is a set of $n$ vertices, $E \subseteq V \times V$ is the set of undirected edges, and $\mathcal{F} = \{f_1, f_2, \dots, f_n\}$ denotes node attributes, with each $f_i \in \mathbb{R}^x$ an attribute vector associated with vertex $v_i$, and $x$ the attribute dimension.
We write $n := |V|$ and $m := |E|$ for the number of vertices and edges, respectively.
For a vertex $u \in V$, its \emph{neighborhood} is $N(u) := \{v \in V : \{u,v\} \in E\}$, and its \emph{degree} is $\deg(u) := |N(u)|$.
Let $A \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}$ denote the adjacency matrix of $G$, where $A_{ij} = 1$ if $\{v_i,v_j\} \in E$ and $A_{ij} = 0$ otherwise.
Let $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the degree vector with $\mathbf{d}_i = \deg(v_i)$.

A \emph{clustering} $C$ of $G$ is a partition of the vertex set $V$ into $k$ disjoint blocks $V_1, \dots, V_k$ such that $V_1 \cup \cdots \cup V_k = V$.
Classical algorithms operate solely on the graph structure $(V,E)$, while learning-based methods additionally exploit node attributes $\mathcal{F}$. 
Learning-based approaches often aim to discover communities that are coherent both in terms of graph connectivity and attribute similarity.

\paragraph*{\textbf{Modularity}.}
The \emph{modularity} score of a clustering is the fraction of edges that connect two vertices within one cluster, minus the expected fraction if the edges were distributed at random.
Let $L_i := |\{\{u,v\} \in E : u,v \in V_i\}|$ denote the number of edges internal to block $V_i$.
The modularity of $C$ is defined as
\[Q(C) := \sum_{i = 1}^{k}{\left(\frac{L_i}{m} - \left(\sum_{u \in V_i}{\frac{\deg(u)}{2m}}\right)^2 \right)} \]
Equivalently, modularity can be expressed in spectral form.
Let $C \in \{0,1\}^{n \times k}$ denote the (hard) cluster assignment matrix, where $C_{ip} = 1$ if vertex $v_i$ belongs to cluster $p$ and $C_{ip} = 0$ otherwise.
Define the \emph{modularity matrix}
$ B := A - \frac{\mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}^\top}{2m}.$
Then modularity can be written as
\[ Q(C) = \frac{1}{2m} \operatorname{Tr}(C^\top B C). \]
This spectral formulation serves as the foundation for many continuous and learning-based approaches.

\paragraph*{\textbf{Graph neural networks}.}
Graph neural networks (GNNs) are a class of neural architectures that compute vector representations for vertices in an attributed graph by iteratively aggregating information from local neighborhoods.
In the context of community detection, GNNs are used to map vertices into a representation space in which structurally or semantically related vertices are close.
Learning-based clustering methods then infer communities indirectly by optimizing objectives such as continuous or differentiable relaxations of modularity, mutual-information maximization,
or contrastive criteria defined over these representations or over derived \emph{soft cluster assignments}, which encode the strength of association between nodes and communities.

We review objective functions and algorithmic paradigms for community detection and introduce the taxonomy used in this work.

Many clustering quality measures have been proposed, including \emph{coverage}, \emph{performance}~\cite{Gaertler2005}, \emph{inter-cluster conductance}~\cite{kannan2004clusterings}, \emph{surprise}~\cite{arnau2004iterative}, and \emph{modularity}~\cite{girvan2002}. Coverage, the fraction of intra-cluster edges, is simple but favors trivial solutions. Modularity mitigates this by comparing observed coverage to its expectation under a null model, and has become one of the most widely used objectives for community detection~\cite{licommunitydetectionsurvey24}. It is the primary quality function in large-scale benchmarks such as the DIMACS challenges~\cite{dimacschallengegraphpartandcluster} and is the focus of this work. While Aref et al.~\cite{survey2023} report that modularity heuristics rarely attain optimal solutions, Ans{\'o}tegui et al.~\cite{maxsat2025} show that the \textsc{VieClus} modularity maximization heuristic routinely computes optimal solutions on small to medium-sized graphs.
%A well-known limitation in modularity is the resolution limit~\cite{resolution2007},which can cause small but well-defined communities to be merged into larger ones. Many modularity-based algorithms provide a \textsc{resolution} parameter to detect smaller communities in large graphs~\cite{louvain2008, leiden2019}. 
Alternative objectives capture complementary notions of community structure: \textit{Weighted Community Cluster} (WCC) maximizes the number of triangles within each community~\cite{wcc2012}, implemented in the \textsc{Scalable Community Detection} algorithm~\cite{scd2014}, and the \textit{Map Equation}, which measures the expected description length of a random walk~\cite{infomap2008}, used in \textsc{Infomap}~\cite{infomap2008}, \textsc{Infoflow}~\cite{infoflow2019}, and the recent learning-based \textsc{Neuromap}~\cite{neuromap2024}. We do not include all the methods based on alternative objective functions in order to maintain our focus on modularity maximization.

Classical modularity-based algorithms use discrete optimization heuristics that consider graph topology only. More recently, learning-based approaches to community detection have been proposed, motivated by the development of GNNs that can model node attributes as feature vectors to capture additional graph information beyond topology alone. 
These methods typically optimize training objectives that are explicitly aligned with modularity or related structural principles.
Compared to classical heuristics, learning-based methods trade exact modularity gains for flexibility, the ability to incorporate node attributes, and increased generalization across graphs.
Despite extensive work in both paradigms, existing surveys do not provide a unified experimental comparison of classical and learning-based modularity optimization methods. Prior surveys typically focus on either classical heuristics~\cite{survey2023} or learning-based methods~\cite{Liu2020,Jin2023,liang2024,Messias2025}.
Li et al.~\cite{Li2024} compare classical and deep learning methods, but limit comparison to Python-based solvers, thus lacking a comprehensive comparison of the state-of-the-art. In addition, algorithmic papers in both paradigms rarely evaluate against methods outside their own class (see Figure~\ref{fig:comparisons}).

Despite high interest from multiple scientific communities and the rapid development of new methods, comparisons between methods remain fragmented. This is a problem for both practitioners who want the best algorithm and for future researchers who need to decide which baseline algorithms to use.

This work addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive experimental comparison of modularity-based community detection algorithms under a unified framework. Here, we provide a taxonomy of the considered methods listed in Table~\ref{tab:comp}. We organize methods by \emph{optimization paradigm}, distinguishing between \emph{classical} combinatorial algorithms and \emph{learning-based} (or GNN-inspired continuous) approaches. Classical methods are further grouped by their dominant optimization mechanism (e.g., greedy, multilevel, or metaheuristic) and by computational setting (e.g., streaming and exact solvers). Learning-based methods are categorized by how modularity is used during training, separating approaches that directly optimize continuous relaxations of modularity from those that use modularity as a guiding signal within broader learning objectives. 
Within each subgroup, algorithms are listed chronologically.

\subsection{Classical Modularity Optimization Methods}

\subsubsection{Greedy agglomeration and multilevel local search}

Early approaches to modularity maximization focused on greedy agglomeration, merging communities to directly increase modularity.
Subsequent work identified limitations of purely agglomerative strategies, notably unbalanced merges and poor scalability, shifting toward multilevel formulations based on local vertex moves.

\textbf{Clauset--Newman--Moore (\textsc{CNM})}~\cite{cnm2004} is a hierarchical agglomerative algorithm that greedily maximizes modularity.
Starting from singleton communities (each vertex assigned to its own cluster), it repeatedly merges the pair of communities with the largest modularity gain.
The algorithm produces a dendrogram representing the full merge hierarchy and returns the clustering attaining maximum modularity. Its running time is $O(md \log n)$, where $d$ is the dendrogram depth.

\textbf{Wakita--Tsurumi (\textsc{WT})}~\cite{wt2007} refines \textsc{CNM} by addressing its tendency to produce highly unbalanced merges that degrade scalability on large graphs.
While preserving the same agglomerative structure, \textsc{WT} modifies merge selection to account for both modularity gain and a community-size consolidation ratio, biasing the process toward more balanced agglomerations and improving scalability.

\textbf{\textsc{Louvain}}~\cite{louvain2008} replaces community-pair merging with greedy local vertex moves.
Vertices are iteratively moved to neighboring communities whenever the induced modularity gain is positive.
Once no improving move exists, communities are contracted into supernodes and the process is repeated, yielding a multilevel optimization scheme.
Each local-moving phase runs in time linear in the number of edges, and the number of multilevel passes is typically small, resulting in near-linear runtime in practice.

\textbf{\textsc{Leiden}}~\cite{leiden2019} addresses \textsc{Louvain}'s tendency to produce disconnected communities by introducing a refinement phase before contraction.
This phase splits and restructures communities through probabilistic, non-greedy merges, while guaranteeing that resulting communities are connected and locally optimal.

\subsubsection{Non-modularity baselines}

We include two widely used non-modularity baselines which rely on random walks to model how information or flow propagates through the network, providing an alternative perspective to modularity optimization.

\textbf{\textsc{Walktrap}}~\cite{walktrap2005} performs hierarchical clustering based on the intuition that short random walks on a graph tend to get ``trapped'' into densely connected regions corresponding to communities. 
Each vertex is characterized by the probability distribution over vertices reached by a random walk starting from it, and a structural similarity distance between vertices (and communities) is defined by comparing these distributions. Communities are then obtained by agglomerative merging of the closest pairs under this distance, producing a dendrogram similar to \textsc{CNM}, which is then cut at the partition attaining maximal modularity. The algorithm runs in $O(m n d)$ time, where $d$ is the depth of the resulting dendrogram.

\textbf{\textsc{Infomap}}~\cite{infomap2008} formulates community detection as an information-theoretic problem, seeking a clustering that minimizes the expected description length of a random walk under the map equation.
It uses a \textsc{Louvain}-inspired greedy multilevel search, augmented with fine- and coarse-tuning steps (submodule and supermodule movements) to escape local minima.

\subsubsection{Metaheuristics and ensemble/hybrid search}

Beyond greedy and multilevel heuristics, several methods apply metaheuristic or ensemble-learning strategies to modularity maximization.
Representative examples include Variable Neighborhood Search (\textsc{VNS})~\cite{vns2012} and its decomposition-based extension \textsc{ADVNDS}~\cite{advnds2019}, which systematically perturb and decompose the current clustering to escape local optima, as well as ensemble-based approaches such as \textsc{CGGCi-RG}~\cite{cggci_rg2012}, which stabilize modularity optimization by consolidating recurring community structures across multiple runs.
These methods combine diversification mechanisms (e.g., randomized perturbations or multiple initializations) with intensification via modularity-based local refinement.
While more computationally expensive than purely greedy algorithms, such strategies often achieve higher modularity by exploring a larger search space.

\textbf{\textsc{COMBO}}~\cite{combo2014} considers pairs of communities and evaluates full redistributions of vertices between them using Kernighan--Lin-style refinements, instead of relocating individual vertices as in \textsc{Louvain}.
By unifying merges, splits, and partial recombinations, \textsc{COMBO} can escape local optima that trap multilevel greedy methods, at the cost of higher computational complexity. The authors report an upper bound on running time of $O(n^2 \log C)$, where $C$ is the number of clusters.

\textbf{\textsc{VieClus}}~\cite{vieclus2018} further extends ensemble-based optimization by adopting a memetic (evolutionary) framework.
Rather than refining a single partition, it maintains a population of clusterings and generates new solutions through recombination and mutation.
Recombination exploits agreement between high-quality solutions via overlay clustering, where two vertices are grouped together only if they co-occur in both parent solutions, while mutation introduces controlled perturbations to preserve diversity.
Each offspring is refined using \textsc{Louvain} or multilevel local search and retained only if it improves modularity. After a set time limit, \textsc{VieClus} returns the best clustering found thus far. 

\subsubsection{Streaming algorithms}

Most classical modularity algorithms assume random access to the full graph, which becomes infeasible for large or dynamically generated networks.
Streaming algorithms address this limitation by processing the graph incrementally under strict memory constraints, typically allowing only a single or few passes over the data.

\textbf{\textsc{Hollocou}}~\cite{hollocou2017} is a one-pass edge-streaming algorithm.
The algorithm starts from the singleton clustering and processes edges sequentially.
For an arriving edge $(u,v)$, \textsc{Hollocou} decides whether one endpoint should join the other's community based on their current community volumes.
If both volumes are below a fixed threshold, the node belonging to the smaller-volume community joins the other.
The authors show that under random stream orderings, this local, volume-based assignment rule corresponds to a greedy optimization of modularity in the streaming setting.

\textbf{\textsc{CluStRE}}~\cite{clustre2025} follows the node-streaming model unlike the edge-streaming model of \textsc{Hollocou}. Here, nodes arrive sequentially together with their neighborhood lists.
Starting from singleton clustering, each node is assigned to the cluster among its neighbors that maximizes the modularity gain similar to a \textsc{Louvain} local move.
In its multi-stage configurations, a quotient graph is constructed during streaming whose nodes represent clusters and whose weighted edges summarize inter- and intra-cluster connectivity, preserving modularity exactly; this quotient graph is optionally refined using \textsc{VieClus}, and the graph may be re-streamed until convergence. In our benchmark, we evaluate the one-pass \emph{light} streaming configuration to measure pure streaming performance.

\subsubsection{Exact solvers}

In contrast to heuristic approaches, exact algorithms provably compute globally optimal modularity clusters.
Since modularity maximization is NP-hard~\cite{Brandes2008}, these methods rely on exponential-time search augmented with bounding techniques or logical formulations such as weighted partial MaxSAT~\cite{maxsat2025}, and are therefore limited to small or medium-sized graphs.

\textbf{\textsc{Bayan}}~\cite{bayan2024} is an exact branch-and-cut algorithm for modularity maximization.
It formulates modularity as an integer program with binary co-membership variables and enforces consistency via triangular constraints on node triples, which branch on whether three nodes must belong to the same community or cannot all be clustered together.
Upper bounds are obtained by solving linear programming relaxations, while lower bounds are computed from feasible clusterings produced by \textsc{COMBO}.
Branching decisions either contract node triples into supernodes or discourage their co-assignment, and the algorithm terminates once optimality is certified or a prescribed optimality gap is reached.

\subsection{Learning-Based Methods}

\subsubsection{Continuous and differentiable modularity maximization}

These approaches replace discrete community assignments with continuous representations, often learned using GNNs, expressing modularity as a continuous or differentiable objective optimized over node representations or soft cluster assignments.

\textbf{\textsc{DMoN}}~\cite{dmon2023} introduces a differentiable relaxation of modularity and realizes it using a GNN.
A GNN followed by a softmax function produces soft cluster assignments, which are optimized using an objective whose main term corresponds to modularity in spectral form. 
The objective further includes a \emph{collapse regularization} term that prevents trivial solutions in which all nodes are assigned to the same cluster.

\textbf{\textsc{GNNS}}~\cite{gnns2022} is a GNN-inspired algorithm that formulates modularity maximization as a recurrent continuous optimization process directly over soft cluster assignments (without trainable neural weights or node features).
Starting from random assignments, node assignments are iteratively updated by combining previous assignments with modularity-weighted aggregation of neighboring assignments.
Activation functions and normalization steps amplify valid assignments supported by positive modularity contributions.
The procedure is repeated for multiple random initializations and parameter choices, and the best converged solution is discretized to obtain the final clustering.

Most learning-based modularity methods, including \textsc{DMoN}, require the number of communities to be specified a priori. \textbf{\textsc{DGCluster}}~\cite{dgcluster2024} eliminates this requirement by learning node embeddings with a GNN and clustering implicitly through a joint optimization objective.
Node embeddings learned by a GNN are projected onto the positive unit hypersphere to induce soft pairwise community memberships, which parameterize a differentiable modularity objective.
A secondary loss optionally incorporates auxiliary local information, such as partial labels or clusterings from classical heuristics, by aligning embedding similarities with a pairwise similarity matrix.
After end-to-end optimization, final communities are obtained by clustering the learned embeddings using \textsc{BIRCH}~\cite{birch1996}.

While \textsc{DGCluster} removes the need to predefine the number of communities, it relies on jointly optimizing multiple objectives which increases model and training complexity.
\textbf{\textsc{LIM}}~\cite{lim2025} addresses this trade-off with a single-objective formulation that still does not require prior specification of the number of communities.
The method first applies \textsc{Louvain} to extract coarse global communities, which are summarized as structural community centers in a feature space.
A graph convolutional network then learns node representations that capture local structural information and node attributes.
Soft cluster assignments are computed by measuring similarity between node representations and the structural centers, and a single modularity objective defined over these assignments is optimized to obtain the final clustering.

\subsubsection{Contrastive/infomax objectives}

These methods do not optimize modularity directly, but instead use it to guide representation learning through alternative objectives.

\textbf{Community Deep Graph Infomax (\textsc{CommDGI})}~\cite{commdgi2020} learns community-aware node representations by jointly maximizing mutual information between nodes and the graph and between nodes and their communities.
Unlike other methods that directly optimize modularity, modularity is used here as an auxiliary objective together with mutual information, via a soft $k$-means-style clustering layer, to encourage structurally coherent communities.

\textbf{Unified Community Detection (\textsc{UCoDe})}~\cite{ucode2023} optimizes a single contrastive objective over the community-wise modularity matrix $C^\top B C$, encouraging high intra-community modularity along the diagonal and low inter-community values off the diagonal.
This contrastive formulation allows \textsc{UCoDe} to recover non-overlapping communities while naturally supporting overlap, without requiring separate objectives or post-processing.
Compared to combined-objective methods such as \textsc{CommDGI}, \textsc{UCoDe} uses a single, interpretable loss and requires minimal hyperparameter tuning.

\textbf{\textsc{MAGI}}~\cite{magi2024} establishes a formal connection between modularity maximization and graph contrastive learning by showing that modularity naturally defines positive and negative node pairs via the sign of the modularity matrix.
Specifically, node pairs with positive modularity coefficients are treated as positive samples, while pairs with non-positive coefficients are treated as negatives, yielding a contrastive objective equivalent to relaxed modularity maximization.
A GNN encoder is trained using a community-aware contrastive loss, with mini-batch modularity matrices constructed via random-walk sampling for scalability.
In our benchmark, we evaluate the authors' scalable GraphSAGE minibatch implementation; their alternative full-graph GCN formulation constructs a dense $n \times n$ affinity matrix, incurring an $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ computational footprint that is prohibitive on larger graphs.

\section{Evaluation Framework}\label{sec:evaluation}
In this section, we describe the benchmarking methodology used to evaluate modularity-based graph clustering algorithms under controlled and reproducible conditions. A central contribution is the integration of 17 clustering algorithms behind a unified benchmarking interface, offering a fair comparison across diverse implementations. In addition to enabling the experiments presented in this work, it will also serve as an openly available resource for future work on graph clustering, simplifying the evaluation of older methods on new data or machines. This section also includes details on the datasets we used to evaluate the clustering algorithms.

\subsection{Benchmarking Interface}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:comp}, over half of the clustering algorithms in the literature lack ready-to-use interfaces for clustering new data. Furthermore, among those with properly documented code, there are wide differences in the input graph format, output formats, and the additional information they report, such as time and memory consumption. This lack of standardization complicates fair benchmarking, as differences in preprocessing, reporting, and resource accounting can substantially affect observed performance.

To address this problem, we aim to provide an interface with a single common input and output format and consistent time and memory monitoring. The interface exposes a common set of inputs, while allowing solver-specific parameters to be provided when applicable:
\[\textsc{Cluster}(G, T_{\max}, p, k, M_{\max}, \mathcal{F})\]
where $G$ is the input graph, $T_{\max}$ is the wall-clock time limit, $p$ is the number of threads available to the algorithm, $k$ is the number of clusters to compute, $M_{\max}$ is the memory limit in gigabytes, and~$\mathcal{F}$ are the graph features. Parameters $p$, $k$, and~$\mathcal{F}$ are only used by a subset of the solvers. From Table~\ref{tab:comp}, parallel solvers use $p$, those that compute a fixed number of clusters use $k$, and any solver that uses graph features can optionally accept $\mathcal{F}$. When $\mathcal{F}$ is not provided by the dataset, but the solver expects features, we generate a 69-dimensional synthetic feature vector (\texttt{ldp+rni}) for each vertex by concatenating a 5-dimensional standardized Local Degree Profile (vertex degree and the minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation of neighbor degrees) with a 64-dimensional seeded Gaussian Random Node Initialization, providing structural and node-distinguishing signals without quadratic memory overhead. Similarly, when a solver requires a target cluster count $k$, labeled \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs provide $|C|$ (or $\max(16, |C|)$ for over-provisioning methods \textsc{DMoN}, \textsc{UCoDe}, and \textsc{GNNS}, following their original evaluation protocols), while unlabeled \textsc{DIMACS} graphs use $k = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$. This interface is designed to capture the most common operational requirements across clustering algorithms while enforcing consistent resource limits and output reporting.

%Regarding code changes, we have tried to make as few modifications to the original source code as possible.
Whenever possible, we use the authors' implementations or the most commonly used library implementation. The exact setup for each algorithm is described in our openly available GitHub repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ClusterBenchmark/ClusterBenchmark}}. We make only minimal, deliberate modifications where necessary to ensure consistent timing, memory accounting, and output quality measures based on the following rules:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Parsing the input and writing the output to a file is not counted in the timing of the algorithm.
    \item For search-based algorithms that always run for the entire $T_{\max}$ duration, the recorded computation time is defined as the point in time when the best solution is found.
    \item Learning-based methods that train a neural network as part of the algorithm will break the training loop at $T_{\max}$, and use the current model at that time to produce \hbox{the final clustering.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Parameter Settings}

Where possible, we use the default configurations for all algorithms. To reflect how algorithms are typically used in practice, our primary evaluation (\textbf{Protocol~A}) deliberately avoids any dataset-specific or instance-specific parameter tuning, benchmarking available implementations out-of-the-box on a single CPU thread with their author-recommended defaults. To isolate the effects of hardware resources and hyperparameter sensitivity, we complement Protocol~A with two targeted protocols: \textbf{Protocol~S} (scalability and accelerators), which evaluates full-machine multi-threaded CPU execution (32 threads) and GPU acceleration while holding algorithm hyperparameters fixed at their defaults; and \textbf{Protocol~B} (per-instance parameter tuning), which runs a 50-trial Bayesian hyperparameter search (via Optuna) maximizing modularity on representative small graphs.

\paragraph*{\textbf{Default configurations (Protocol~A).}}
Among classical and topology-only methods (\textsc{CNM} exposes no tunable parameters), \textsc{Louvain}, \textsc{COMBO}, \textsc{Leiden} ($\beta = 0.01$, $2$ iterations), and \textsc{Bayan} (using the Gurobi LP/ILP solver~\cite{gurobi} with optimality gap $10^{-3}$) all use a default resolution of $1$. \textsc{Walktrap} uses a random-walk length of $4$, \textsc{Infomap} uses $1$ outer trial, \textsc{Hollocou} sets the maximum volume threshold to $v_{\max} = 10\,000$, \textsc{CluStRE} runs in its one-pass light streaming mode optimizing modularity, \textsc{VieClus} runs with a $1$-hour time limit, and \textsc{GNNS} uses the \textsc{GNNS100} configuration ($100$ random initializations, keep fraction $1/3$). \subsection{Dataset}

We consider three datasets commonly used in the literature. The first set consists of 10 real-world graphs with known community labels; we refer to this dataset as the \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs. The machine learning algorithms commonly use these graphs, and the details of the instances are shown in Table~\ref{tab:ground_truth_graphs}. Next, we consider the instances used in the 10th DIMACS implementation challenge on graph clustering~\cite{dimacschallengegraphpartandcluster}; we call this dataset \textsc{DIMACS} graphs. This dataset is popular among the classical modularity optimization methods, and the details of these graphs are shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:instances}. Finally, we use synthetically generated graphs with node features generated using the attributed, degree-corrected stochastic block model (ADC-SBM)~\cite{adcsbm2022}. This generator plants $k$ clusters in the graph, and generates random edges via a distribution that is conditional on those clusters. It does so using the two input parameters $d$ and $d_{out} \leq d$, where $d$ is the expected degree for each vertex and $d_{out}$ is the expected number of neighbors for a node in a different cluster than its own. The ADC-SBM generator also produces node features that are correlated with the community structure. It does so using the two input parameters $\sigma$ for intra-cluster variance and $\sigma_c$ for cluster center variance.

In this section, we present benchmark results obtained using the unified benchmarking interface described in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. We report results on real-world graphs with and without ground-truth labels, as well as on synthetically generated graphs with controlled structural and feature noise. Rather than exhaustively ranking algorithms, these experiments illustrate how different algorithmic design choices behave under a standardized benchmarking setup. To support this, we also provide a dynamic website reporting updated results\footnote{\url{https://clusterbenchmark.github.io/}}.

\subsection{Computational Environment}

All algorithms are evaluated on identical hardware nodes to eliminate variability and ensure fair comparisons. Each machine is equipped with a 16-core (32-thread) Intel Xeon w5-3435X processor, 125~GiB of system memory, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~4090 GPU with 24~GiB of VRAM (driver version 560.35.03, CUDA~12.6), running Ubuntu~24.04.3~LTS with Linux kernel 6.8.0-107. Compiled C/C++ implementations are built with GCC~13.3.0, and Python implementations run on Python~3.12.13.

To prevent resource contention and cross-talk, each benchmark job runs exclusively on a node under Linux \texttt{cgroup} memory tracking. Under our primary out-of-the-box evaluation (\textbf{Protocol~A}), all 17 algorithms are restricted to a single CPU thread ($p = 1$, \texttt{device=cpu}) to establish a uniform sequential baseline across both single-threaded and parallel implementations. Under \textbf{Protocol~S}, we evaluate the parallel and neural methods using all 32 hardware threads on CPU ($p = 32$, \texttt{device=cpu}) as well as GPU acceleration ($p = 32$, \texttt{device=cuda}) for CUDA-enabled architectures. Each algorithm--instance run is subject to a wall-clock time limit of one hour ($3\,600$~s) and a host memory limit of 120~GB (or the 24~GiB VRAM ceiling when executing on the GPU). Runs exceeding the memory limit are terminated and recorded as \emph{memory limit exceeded} (MLE). Time limits include a 50\% watchdog grace period ($4\,500$~s) so that fixed-epoch neural training loops can terminate cleanly; runs exceeding the one-hour budget are recorded as \emph{time limit exceeded} (TLE).

For stochastic methods, we execute five independent runs seeded by the run index and report the best solution quality achieved. In addition to modularity (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary}), we evaluate ground-truth community recovery using Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)~\cite{danon2005comparing} and an edge-restricted pairwise F1 score. The F1 score is computed over adjacent vertex pairs: an edge $(u,v)$ is a true positive (TP) if $u$ and $v$ are assigned to the same cluster by the algorithm as well as in the ground-truth partition. Defining false positives (FP) and false negatives (FN) analogously over graph edges, $F1 := \frac{2\mathit{TP}}{2\mathit{TP} + \mathit{FP} + \mathit{FN}}$.

\paragraph*{\textbf{Relative performance distributions.}}
To compare multiple algorithms across heterogeneous benchmark instances without sensitive raw-score averaging, we follow the relative-ratio normalization of Dolan and Mor{\'e}~\cite{perf2002}. For each benchmark instance and quality metric, an algorithm's score is normalized by the best score observed across all evaluated methods on that instance, yielding a relative quality ratio $\tau \in [0, 1]$. Rather than superimposing 16 cumulative step functions that cross when algorithms time out, we display a horizontal strip lane for each algorithm in which every solved instance is plotted as an individual marker at its ratio $\tau$ (with small vertical jitter to separate overlapping points near $\tau = 1$), and unresolved instances (TLE/MLE) are shown as $\times$ markers in a dedicated \emph{Fail} band on the right. Algorithms whose markers cluster tightly near $\tau = 1$ with few or no entries in the \emph{Fail} band achieve both high solution quality and broad instance coverage.

\subsection{State-of-the-Art Comparisons (Protocol~A)}

Figures~\ref{fig:perf_quality} and~\ref{fig:perf_gt_recovery} present the out-of-the-box Protocol~A performance profiles for modularity optimization and ground-truth community recovery, respectively. Across both the attributed \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs and the topology-only \textsc{DIMACS} suite (Figure~\ref{fig:perf_quality}), classical modularity heuristics clearly dominate pure modularity maximization. \textsc{VieClus} achieves the highest modularity on every single instance it completes ($\tau = 1.0$ on 9/10 \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs and 30/32 \textsc{DIMACS} graphs), consistent with prior findings~\cite{vieclus2018,maxsat2025}, failing only on the largest instances (\texttt{ogbn-products}, \texttt{uk-2002}, and \texttt{uk-2007-05}) due to time or memory limits. \textsc{Leiden} and \textsc{Louvain} follow closely behind: \textsc{Leiden} solves all 10 \textsc{Ground-Truth} instances with $\tau \ge 0.990$ and 31/32 \textsc{DIMACS} instances with $\tau \ge 0.953$. Among continuous and learning-based methods, only \textsc{DGCluster} ($\tau \ge 0.923$ on 8/10 \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs) and the topology-driven \textsc{GNNS} ($\tau \ge 0.925$ on 10/10 \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs and 28/32 \textsc{DIMACS} graphs) approach the modularity of classical heuristics, whereas contrastive and deep GNN models lag substantially in raw modularity, particularly on the featureless \textsc{DIMACS} graphs where they rely on synthetic degree-profile and random-node-initialization (\texttt{ldp+rni}) features.

When evaluating ground-truth community recovery on the \textsc{Ground-Truth} dataset (Figure~\ref{fig:perf_gt_recovery}), the relative standing shifts markedly. Here, attribute-aware learning-based methods are highly competitive with—and frequently surpass—pure modularity maximizers. In terms of NMI (Figure~\ref{fig:perf_gt_recovery}, top), \textsc{MAGI} achieves the strongest overall profile (mean NMI of $0.533$ across 9/10 solved instances, with $\tau \ge 0.859$ on 80\% of instances), while \textsc{CommDGI}, \textsc{DGCluster}, and \textsc{UCoDe} each achieve the best NMI ($\tau = 1.0$) on multiple individual networks alongside \textsc{Leiden}. On edge-restricted Pairwise F1 (Figure~\ref{fig:perf_gt_recovery}, bottom), \textsc{MAGI}, \textsc{LIM}, \textsc{CommDGI}, \textsc{DGCluster}, \textsc{UCoDe}, and the flow-based \textsc{Infomap} all attain $\tau = 1.0$ on top instances. This divergence between Figure~\ref{fig:perf_quality} and Figure~\ref{fig:perf_gt_recovery} underscores a fundamental misalignment between topological modularity and externally defined node labels: for example, on Citeseer, the ground-truth label partition has a modularity of only $0.54$, whereas classical and neural modularity maximizers find partitions with modularity up to $0.89$. Incorporating node attributes allows GNNs to align communities with semantic categories even when citation edges cross category boundaries.

The streaming algorithms \textsc{Hollocou} and \textsc{CluStRE} trade solution quality for extreme scalability, solving all 10 \textsc{Ground-Truth} and all 32 \textsc{DIMACS} graphs—including \texttt{uk-2007-05} ($3.3$~billion edges)—with \textsc{CluStRE} consistently outperforming \textsc{Hollocou} in both modularity and NMI/F1. On the eight smallest \textsc{DIMACS} instances where exact optimal modularity partitions are known~\cite{bayan2024,maxsat2025}, \textsc{VieClus} recovers the optimal modularity on all 8/8 instances, \textsc{Leiden} and \textsc{GNNS} on 6/8, \textsc{COMBO} on 5/8, and \textsc{Louvain} and \textsc{DGCluster} on 2/8.

Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency} summarizes the trade-off between solution quality and execution time under Protocol~A using geometric means across solved instances, with per-graph runtimes normalized by the number of edges ($\mu\text{s}/m$). Across both datasets, the algorithms span six orders of magnitude in normalized runtime. Streaming methods (\textsc{Hollocou} at $0.04$--$0.05\,\mu\text{s}/m$ and \textsc{CluStRE} at $0.26$--$0.64\,\mu\text{s}/m$) occupy the extreme low-latency regime, whereas multi-level local-moving heuristics (\textsc{Leiden} at $0.78$--$0.85\,\mu\text{s}/m$ and \textsc{Louvain} at $1.74$--$2.12\,\mu\text{s}/m$) dominate the Pareto frontier, achieving near-optimal modularity more than four orders of magnitude faster than the evolutionary solver \textsc{VieClus} (which intentionally exhausts its configurable time budget to refine partitions). Among continuous and learning-based methods, the sparse matrix-free \textsc{GNNS} ($375\,\mu\text{s}/m$ on \textsc{Ground-Truth}) is an order of magnitude faster than deep neural architectures ($4\,300$--$26\,400\,\mu\text{s}/m$), which incur substantial overhead from multi-epoch gradient optimization.

Peak memory consumption exhibits an equally sharp divide between algorithmic paradigms (detailed per-instance measurements are provided in Appendix~\ref{sec:memory}, Table~\ref{tab:memory}). Streaming methods process edges in a single pass with minimal state, requiring only $1.1$~GB on \texttt{ogbn-products} ($61.9$~M edges), while \textsc{Leiden} and \textsc{Louvain} require $12.7$~GB. In contrast, learning-based GNNs store high-dimensional node embeddings and intermediate activation tensors, consuming $14$--$47$~GB on medium-to-large graphs. Furthermore, implementations that materialize dense $O(n^2)$ modularity or similarity matrices in host memory—specifically \textsc{LIM}, \textsc{UCoDe}, and \textsc{CommDGI}—attempt to allocate $214$~GiB on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv} ($n = 169\,343$) and $43.6$~TiB on \texttt{ogbn-products}, resulting in immediate memory exhaustion (\textsc{MLE}).

To quantify how multi-core parallelism and hardware accelerators shift this efficiency frontier, \textbf{Protocol~S} evaluates the parallel and learning-based solvers using all 32 hardware threads on CPU ($p = 32$, \texttt{device=cpu}) and on the NVIDIA RTX~4090 GPU ($p = 32$, \texttt{device=cuda}); complete per-instance runtimes are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:par-seq} (Table~\ref{tab:par-seq}). Expanding from 1 to 32 CPU threads yields geometric-mean speedups of $8.0\times$ for \textsc{UCoDe}, $7.6\times$ for \textsc{LIM}, $5.0\times$ for \textsc{DGCluster}, $3.7\times$ for \textsc{CommDGI}, and $3.4\times$ for \textsc{DMoN} across medium-scale networks. Offloading training to the RTX~4090 GPU produces dramatic additional accelerations over single-threaded CPU execution, reaching geometric-mean speedups of $158\times$ for \textsc{LIM} (e.g., reducing \texttt{coauthor-physics} from $3\,615$~s to $40.5$~s), $100\times$ for \textsc{UCoDe}, $95\times$ for \textsc{CommDGI}, $37\times$ for \textsc{DGCluster}, $13\times$ for \textsc{GNNS}, and $12\times$ for \textsc{DMoN}. Crucially, inspection of completed training epochs (Appendix~\ref{sec:memory}, Table~\ref{tab:iterations}) shows that while most neural methods already finish all requested epochs within one hour on smaller graphs under single-threaded CPU execution, larger networks such as \texttt{coauthor-physics} and \texttt{ogbn-arxiv} prematurely truncate training under $p=1$ (e.g., \textsc{DGCluster} completes $270/300$ epochs on \texttt{ogbn-arxiv}, and \textsc{LIM} completes $111/300$ on \texttt{coauthor-physics}) but finish 100\% of their training epochs in under two minutes on the GPU. However, GPU acceleration introduces a stricter memory bottleneck: because the RTX~4090 is limited to 24~GiB of VRAM compared to 120~GB of system RAM, full-graph GNNs hit out-of-memory limits on smaller graphs on the GPU than on the CPU (for instance, \textsc{CommDGI} requires $29.7$~GB on \texttt{coauthor-physics} and therefore succeeds on CPU but triggers \textsc{MLE} on GPU).

To assess how strongly algorithm rankings depend on hyperparameter tuning versus out-of-the-box defaults, \textbf{Protocol~B} conducts 50-trial Bayesian optimization using Optuna~\cite{optuna2019} on the three canonical citation networks (Cora, Citeseer, and Pubmed), maximizing modularity $Q$. Table~\ref{tab:protocol_b} compares default and tuned modularity scores across methods with tunable algorithmic or loss parameters.

The results reveal a sharp dichotomy in hyperparameter robustness. Classical modularity-maximizing heuristics (\textsc{Leiden}, \textsc{Louvain}, and \textsc{COMBO}) exhibit virtually zero sensitivity ($\lvert\Delta Q\rvert \le 0.005$), because their default resolution parameter ($\gamma = 1.0$) already aligns directly with standard Newman--Girvan modularity; on Pubmed, tuning \textsc{COMBO}'s resolution and community cap also enables it to finish well within the one-hour limit ($Q = 0.740$). For \textsc{Walktrap}, tuning the random-walk length $t \in [2, 12]$ yields consistent moderate gains ($+0.016$ to $+0.028$). Enabling multi-pass refinement in \textsc{CluStRE} (\texttt{clustre-strong}) lifts its modularity by $+0.268$ to $+0.393$ over single-pass streaming (\texttt{clustre-fast}), bringing it within $0.005$ of \textsc{Leiden} on all three citation networks.

Among learning-based methods, architectures whose loss functions balance multiple competing terms—or that are sensitive to learning rate and temperature scaling—benefit substantially from tuning. \textsc{GNNS} gains $+0.015$ to $+0.028$ by tuning its subspace rank and step count, while full-graph \textsc{MAGI} (GCN) improves by $+0.049$ to $+0.106$ and mini-batch \textsc{MAGI} (GraphSAGE) improves by $+0.049$ on Pubmed. Most strikingly, \textsc{CommDGI}—which optimizes a joint combination of deep graph infomax, $k$-means clustering temperature, and modularity—suffers from mode collapse under default parameters on Pubmed ($Q = 0.003$ at $k = \lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil = 141$, and $Q = 0.484$ at exact $k=3$), but recovers $Q = 0.690$ after tuning its loss weights and cluster temperature. Notably, however, even after 50 trials of per-instance Bayesian optimization, no learning-based algorithm surpasses the untuned out-of-the-box modularity of \textsc{VieClus} or \textsc{Leiden} on any of the three networks.

To isolate how clustering algorithms trade off topological structure against vertex attributes, we evaluate them on synthetic attributed networks generated using our ADC-SBM generator (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). Rather than ranking solvers, these experiments reveal how different architectures respond to controlled degradation of structural and attribute signals. We conduct two high-resolution parameter sweeps on graphs with $n = 1\,000$ vertices, $k = 4$ clusters, average degree $d = 20$, and intra-cluster feature spread $\sigma = 1$: (i)~a \emph{structural degradation} sweep that increases the expected external degree $d_{\text{out}}$ from $1$ to $14$ in steps of $0.25$ while holding cluster features well-separated ($\sigma_c = 3$), and (ii)~a \emph{feature degradation} sweep that varies the cluster center standard deviation $\sigma_c$ logarithmically from $10^{-2}$ (uninformative overlapping features) to $10^{1}$ (well-separated features) while holding the graph topology strongly modular ($d_{\text{out}} = 2$).

Figure~\ref{fig:adcsbm} summarizes both experiments. Under structural degradation (Figure~\ref{fig:adcsbm}, left), topology-only methods (\textsc{VieClus}, \textsc{Leiden}, and \textsc{GNNS}) maintain near-perfect recovery up to $d_{\text{out}} \approx 8$, after which they undergo a sharp phase transition as the stochastic block model approaches its detectability limit, dropping below $\text{NMI} = 0.11$ by $d_{\text{out}} = 11$. In contrast, attribute-aware GNNs leverage the informative node features ($\sigma_c = 3$) to sustain recovery well into the structurally noisy regime: at $d_{\text{out}} = 11$, \textsc{DMoN} achieves $\text{NMI} = 0.849$, \textsc{MAGI} (GCN) achieves $0.767$, \textsc{CommDGI} achieves $0.724$, and \textsc{DGCluster} achieves $0.720$.

Comparing the two variants of \textsc{MAGI}—our default two-stage GraphSAGE encoder (\textsc{MAGI}-SAGE) versus the single-stage full-graph GCN encoder (\textsc{MAGI}-GCN)—exposes a striking architectural trade-off in message passing. Under structural degradation (Figure~\ref{fig:adcsbm}, left), \textsc{MAGI}-SAGE maintains $\text{NMI} = 1.000$ across the entire sweep up to $d_{\text{out}} = 14$, because GraphSAGE explicitly concatenates each vertex's ego-features with its sampled neighborhood representation, preserving clean ego-attribute separability even when $70\%$ of incident edges cross cluster boundaries. Conversely, under feature degradation (Figure~\ref{fig:adcsbm}, right), when attributes become pure noise ($\sigma_c \le 0.1$) on a clean graph topology ($d_{\text{out}} = 2$), \textsc{MAGI}-SAGE collapses to $\text{NMI} \approx 0.01$--$0.02$, whereas \textsc{MAGI}-GCN retains $\text{NMI} = 0.489$--$0.789$ because full-graph degree-normalized Laplacian averaging smooths out zero-mean feature noise across well-connected communities. A similar spectrum appears across the other GNNs: \textsc{DGCluster} relies primarily on its pairwise modularity-similarity matrix and retains $\text{NMI} = 1.000$ across all $\sigma_c \in [10^{-2}, 10^{1}]$ alongside \textsc{VieClus} and \textsc{GNNS}, while \textsc{DMoN} ($\text{NMI} = 0.607$ at $\sigma_c = 0.01$ and $0.474$ at $d_{\text{out}} = 14$) and \textsc{CommDGI} ($0.574$ at $\sigma_c = 0.01$) provide a more balanced compromise between topology and attributes. (We omit \textsc{LIM} from Figure~\ref{fig:adcsbm} as it frequently collapses to a single cluster on these synthetic instances.)

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a comprehensive empirical benchmark of 17 modularity-based graph clustering algorithms spanning exact mathematical programming, classical greedy and random-walk heuristics, streaming algorithms, parallel shared-memory heuristics, and recent deep learning architectures. When pure modularity maximization is the objective, classical combinatorial methods remain the clear state of the art: the evolutionary solver \textsc{VieClus} achieves the highest modularity on every instance it completes across both attributed and featureless benchmark suites, while \textsc{Leiden} matches \textsc{VieClus} within $1\%$ modularity on the \textsc{Ground-Truth} graphs and executes more than four orders of magnitude faster.

At the same time, our multi-protocol evaluation clarifies where alternative paradigms excel and where fundamental bottlenecks remain:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scalability vs.\ quality:} Single-pass streaming methods (\textsc{CluStRE} and \textsc{Hollocou}) process billion-edge graphs such as \texttt{uk-2007-05} ($3.3$~billion edges) within a few gigabytes of memory, and enabling multi-pass refinement in \textsc{CluStRE} closes nearly the entire modularity gap to \textsc{Leiden} on medium-scale networks.
    \item \textbf{Ground-truth community recovery:} Because real-world semantic labels frequently diverge from purely topological modularity, attribute-aware neural methods (\textsc{MAGI}, \textsc{DGCluster}, \textsc{CommDGI}, and \textsc{UCoDe}) match or outperform classical heuristics in NMI and Pairwise F1 on attributed networks. Moreover, our synthetic ADC-SBM experiments demonstrate that architectural choices—such as ego-feature concatenation in GraphSAGE versus Laplacian smoothing in GCNs—govern whether a neural method is resilient to structural noise or attribute noise.
    \item \textbf{Hardware acceleration and tuning sensitivity:} While GPU acceleration under Protocol~S speeds up neural training by $12\times$--$158\times$ and enables full epoch convergence on medium-sized graphs, current GNN implementations remain constrained by $O(n^2)$ dense matrix allocations in host memory or the 24~GiB VRAM ceiling on GPUs. Furthermore, Protocol~B demonstrates that whereas classical modularity heuristics work out of the box without tuning, multi-loss neural architectures often require careful hyperparameter optimization to avoid mode collapse, and still do not surpass untuned classical heuristics in raw modularity.
\end{itemize}

To support ongoing research, we release our open-source benchmarking harness, standardized dataset pipelines, and containerized solver wrappers. By decoupling algorithmic evaluation from ad-hoc preprocessing, cluster-count assumptions, and hardware disparities, we hope this framework establishes a rigorous foundation for developing the next generation of scalable, attribute-aware community detection methods.
